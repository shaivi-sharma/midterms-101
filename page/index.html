01

What is a midterm, exactly?

The vote halfway through a president's four years. Think of it as a report card.

A president serves four years. Two years in, the country votes again, but not for president. It picks the people who write the laws. Because it lands in the middle of a presidential term, it's called a midterm.

It works like a report card on whoever runs the White House, and the grades are usually rough. Since World War II, the president's party has lost House seats in every midterm except two (1998 and 2002). 2026 was not an exception.

The midterm splits a presidential term in half. What voters decided on Nov. 3 shapes the last two years.

02

If not the president, who's on the ballot?

Congress, governors, and a long tail of local jobs and yes-or-no questions.

The president's name doesn't appear anywhere. A typical ballot this year looked something like this, from the top down.

03

What is Congress, and why two parts?

Two chambers that must pass the exact same bill before it can become law.

Congress is the country's legislature, its law-making body. It has two chambers. A bill has to pass both, word for word, before it goes to the president.

The House of Representatives has 435 members. Bigger states get more seats. Every seat is up every two years, so the whole House can turn over in one night. Whichever party wins 218 seats runs it: it picks the Speaker, controls every committee, and decides what gets investigated. Spending bills traditionally start here.

The Senate has 100 members, two per state, whether the state is California or Wyoming. Senators serve six years, staggered so only about a third are up at once. Think of a shared house where one housemate moves out every two years and the rest stay. This year 35 seats were up. The Senate also approves the president's judges and cabinet picks.

A Senate majority is 51 seats. At 50–50, the vice president breaks ties, so the White House's party needs only 50. One more catch: most bills need 60 Senate votes just to get past a filibuster, so even a majority can't pass everything alone.

04

Isn't the president in charge?

Of the government, yes. Of the money and the laws, no.

The president is elected separately from Congress, so the two can be run by rival parties. Think of a company where the CEO runs the place day to day, but the board signs off on the budget and big hires. A president can't pass laws, set the budget or appoint judges without Congress.

There are three keys: the House, the Senate and the presidency. A law needs all three. A party that holds all three has a trifecta. Lose even one and you have a divided government, where each side can block the other.

05

Where did things stand going in?

Republicans held all three keys. Democrats needed one to slow them down.

Going into Nov. 3, Republicans held the presidency, the House and the Senate. For two years that meant very little pushback from Congress.

The House was split 218 Republicans to 214 Democrats, plus one independent who votes with Republicans and two empty seats. Democrats needed only a handful of seats. To take the Senate, held 53–47, they needed a net gain of 4 to reach 51, because at 50–50 the Republican vice president breaks the tie.

History favored Democrats in the House. The map seemed to favor Republicans in the Senate: 22 of the 35 seats up were Republican-held, many in states Donald Trump won easily in 2024. That's not how it went. See the results.

06

What changes if the other side wins?

Winning Congress doesn't let you rule. It lets you block, and ask questions.

The president can veto any bill. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers: 290 House members and 67 senators. So a new majority mostly gains the power to stop things and to investigate.

With the House: block the president's bills, write the first version of the budget, open investigations, and subpoena documents and testimony. The House can also impeach, which is like filing charges.

With the Senate: reject the president's judges and cabinet picks, and hold the trial after an impeachment. Removing a president takes 67 senators, two-thirds of the Senate. That has never happened.

07

How do you read the results?

"Called" is not the same as "counted," and neither is the same as "official."

Polls close state by state, from 6 p.m. Eastern in parts of Kentucky and Indiana to 1 a.m. in Alaska. News organizations, led by the Associated Press, call a race when the trailing candidate can no longer catch up, often long before every vote is counted.

Leads can flip. Some states count mail ballots first, others last, so a candidate can lead early and lose by morning. That isn't a sign of fraud. It's the order the votes are counted in. Very close races can take weeks: a week after this election, Alaska and Mississippi still had no winner in their Senate races.

Nothing is official until each state certifies its results, weeks later. The new Congress is sworn in on Jan. 3, 2027. Until then, the old one stays in charge.

08

Words you'll hear

Called
A news organization projects the winner because the trailing candidate can't catch up. Not official yet.
Flip
A seat changes from one party to the other.
Incumbent
The person who already holds the seat and is running to keep it.
Too close to call
The gap is smaller than the votes left to count. It's a wait, not a tie.
Not up
Senate seats not on the ballot this year. Those senators still count toward control.
Trifecta
One party holds the presidency, the House and the Senate.
Filibuster
A Senate rule that means most bills need 60 votes to move forward, not 51.
Ranked-choice
Voters rank candidates. If nobody gets a majority, last place drops out and their votes move to the next choice. Alaska and Maine use it.
Runoff
A second election between the top two when nobody clears a required share. Louisiana has two House runoffs on Dec. 12.
Lame duck
The weeks between the election and Jan. 3, when the old Congress is still in office.
Certification
Each state formally signs off on its count. This is when results become official.
